How We Relate to Our Environment

Much of what passes between us happens before either of us says a word. A child takes in the pace of our movements, the look on our face and the sound of our voice. We pick up their response in much the same way. Each of us is sensing and responding, and what each of us conveys becomes part of the other person’s environment. The Shift Map explores nine ways this happens in everyday life.

We are broadcasting and receiving all the time. We take in signals from the environment around us, and we broadcast our response through our breathing, our movements, our face and our voice. What we broadcast becomes part of the environment others receive. What they convey becomes part of ours. It’s a constant back and forth.

Benign Authority

Benign authority grows as you understand the effect of the environment on your child and take responsibility for creating an environment that supports their maturity. You provide secure, predictable parameters within which your child can settle, take part and express the sociality they were born with.

As their innate biological expectations are met, the behaviour that drew your attention can begin to settle. Your child has more room to come to you, look to you for direction and follow willingly.

Worthy and welcome

There’s an atmosphere in a home that you can feel. When the sense that everyone is worthy and welcome is palpable, the child’s innate sociality becomes available to them.

To feel worthy is to be seen as capable and competent, and to feel capable and competent in yourself. To feel welcome is to be trusted and valued, and to come to trust and value yourself.

As the parent takes responsibility for creating that environment, they come to occupy the seat of benign authority. The child can come alongside them, look to them for direction and take part of their own accord. Helping, following and joining in become second nature, part of the ordinary flow of life together.

What I mean by joining up

I remember taking my daughters to see the horseman Monty Roberts work with an untamed horse. It had never worn a saddle or carried a rider. As I watched, I thought about the old idea that a horse had to be broken before anyone could ride it. The aim was to get the horse to give up resisting. I see something of that same assumption in modern parenting, where we can begin with the idea that children need taming and come to read their submission as cooperation.

Monty stood inside a round pen with the horse. I watched him turn his body sideways as it circled. Over time, the horse lowered its head, drew closer and eventually came to stand beside him. Monty called this Join-Up. In that same session, I watched him saddle the horse for the first time in its life and then mount it. What stayed with me was the order of events. The horse had already taken those last steps towards him before the saddle went on.

I think of my daughters standing beside me while I made a fire. They were already part of what was happening. I’d ask them to pass me something and they would. I saw the same willingness when we began taking our shoes off at home. I slipped mine off and they followed suit. Before long, they were asking their friends to take theirs off too. It had become part of how we lived together.

That’s what I mean when I say a child is joined up with you. There’s enough safety and certainty in the relationship for them to come alongside you, look to you for direction and take part of their own accord.

When is it easy for your child to come alongside you and join in? What’s the atmosphere like between you in those moments?

What is mine to carry?

Every parent has worries. You may be anxious about money, work or a relationship. Those worries can still be with you when your child walks into the room.

Part of benign authority is making a conscious distinction between what’s appropriate to share with your child and what belongs with you as the adult. A change in what the family can afford may be something your child needs to know about. The worry about how to pay the bill is yours to carry and, if you need support, to share with another adult.

What BAP is

Biologically Aligned Parenting starts from one position. Your child arrives exquisitely social. They aren't a blank slate waiting to be shaped. They're more like a seed that already knows it will become an oak tree, as long as the conditions are there. Every child is born with five innate expectations, and these are required for that inbuilt sociality to express itself. Your child is primed to become capable and competent, and so to be trusted and valued. The ideal environment is pregnant with one notion: everyone here is worthy and welcome. Worthy means capable and competent. Welcome means trusted and valued. And it's your child's body, not their intellect, that reads the environment. It picks up how you show up in everyday life, among them your voice, your face, your touch, your breath and the rhythm of your home. Your child doesn't receive your words so much as the whole of you.

How it differs from modern parenting

Modern parenting manages behaviour. It offers strategies, scripts and consequences, all aimed at the signal and not the cause. None of us chose this. We absorbed it through informed conditioning: from how we were raised, from family and friends, from experts, books and the culture around us. It's so ordinary that nobody questions it. Take the most accepted notion of all, that children don't come with a manual. They do. It's written in their biology, not in words. Their behaviour is them reading from it, and telling you when what they expect isn't arriving.
