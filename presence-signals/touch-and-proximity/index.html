Within Reach

Touch and proximity, and what your nearness conveys

“Not now, I’m busy”

Your child drifts into the kitchen while you’re cooking and leans against your leg. Or they climb onto the sofa and press up against you while you’re answering an email. Or, older now, they hang about in the doorway of the room you’re in, not saying much. And without quite noticing, you shift away, or say “Give me some space”, or carry on as though they weren’t there.

It’s such an ordinary moment that it hardly registers. But very often, what the child was reaching for wasn’t a conversation. It was you, nearby.

Touch and proximity is one of the nine presence signals in the Shift Map: nine presence signals, five innate expectations, four instinctive responses. It carries physical closeness, and the quality of it: whether your touch is easy or rushed, whether you are near enough to be felt, and whether your body says “you’re welcome here” or “not now”.

A note on language. When I say a child “reads”, “hears” or “expects” something, I don’t mean they are thinking it. A child isn’t weighing up who is in charge or whether you’re paying attention. These are biological processes, happening well below thought. I’ve put them into human words so we can talk about them.

What the research says about touch and nearness

Jean Liedloff saw it in the Amazon long before the laboratories caught up: babies carried in arms, close to a moving adult, were calm. In 2013 Gianluca Esposito, Kumi Kuroda and their colleagues measured it. They recorded the heart rate, movement and crying of twelve babies aged one to six months as their mothers sat holding them or carried them while walking. When their mothers walked with them, the babies stopped crying and moving almost at once, and their heart rates fell quickly, far more than when they were simply held by a mother sitting down. The researchers found the same calming response in mouse pups being carried, and saw it as a basic part of how mammal mothers and babies work together.

Touch can hold a baby through a difficult moment too. In the still face studies we met in Where Your Eyes Go, a mother’s face going blank distresses her baby. But Dale Stack and Darwin Muir found that when mothers touched their five month olds during the still face, the babies grimaced less and smiled more than babies whose mothers didn’t touch them. The face was absent. The touch was still there, and it carried them.

The effects can last. Ruth Feldman and her colleagues asked 73 mothers of premature babies to hold them skin to skin for one hour a day for two weeks, and compared them with 73 matched babies who had standard incubator care. They then followed the children seven times over ten years. At ten, the children who had been held skin to skin had more organised sleep, a better regulated response to stress, more mature nervous system functioning and better thinking skills. Two weeks of touch, at the very start, still showing a decade later.

And touch seems to shape the social brain. Jens Brauer and his colleagues in Leipzig watched 43 five year olds play with their mothers for ten minutes and counted how often the mothers touched them. The children who were touched more showed more resting activity in the brain’s “social” network, the areas involved in understanding other people, and more connection between its parts.

The research on touch with older children and teenagers is much thinner. What we can say is that the need for nearness doesn’t vanish. It changes shape.

What distance or tense touch conveys

In the Shift Map, touch and proximity mainly carries the first of the five innate expectations: contact, not separation. It also carries watching, not watched, because a child who is near you, getting on with their own thing, is being part of your life rather than the centre of it.

Easy, unhurried nearness conveys that the child belongs here, close to an adult who is steady. Touch that is quick, tense or functional, the hand that steers rather than holds, conveys something else. So does a body that keeps turning away, or a parent who is always across the room, or behind a closed door, or on the far side of a screen. None of this is said. It’s carried in the space between you.

A child’s biology registers this well below thought. And what follows isn’t the child testing you. It’s their biology signalling that something it expects is missing. Like the castaway waving and shouting at a ship on the horizon, it works to correct what’s missing, or to find some relief from the discomfort and stress of not having it. And if the ship sails past without seeing them, the waving eventually stops. Not because nothing is missing any more, but because signalling hasn’t worked. The biology turns to coping with the absence instead, and that can look like sadness, withdrawal or something close to depression. That, too, is the biology doing its best with what’s missing.

So when this signal is off, children tend to respond in one of the four instinctive ways. Some fight, becoming rough, grabbing or clambering on you at the worst moment. Some take flight, disappearing into their room. Some freeze, going still and self-contained, as though they’ve stopped expecting to be held. And some fawn, clinging, following you from room to room, anxious to stay close. None of these is a decision made against you. Each is the biology trying either to put things right or to get relief.

Age and stage

The research in this piece looked at babies of one to six months, five month olds, premature babies followed to ten and five year olds. There’s much less research on touch with older children and teenagers. The principle holds at every age: nearness settles. What it looks like changes as a child grows, and an older child’s touch should always be on their terms, welcome rather than taken. These are illustrations, not scripts.

Age What tends to happen Settled nearness might look like
Babies, up to about 6 months Being carried by a moving adult calms the whole body, heart rate included. Touch can carry them through a moment when your face is elsewhere. Carried in arms or a sling while you get on with things, not only held still.
Around 5 How often they’re touched in ordinary play is linked with how their social brain develops. A hand on the back, a lap to climb onto, closeness folded into ordinary moments.
Older children and teenagers Less research. Touch often becomes something they offer or refuse. The need for nearness tends to stay. Being around: in the kitchen, in the car, in the same room, with touch when it’s welcome.

What this means in your home

Modern parenting has quietly built distance into ordinary life. Babies are put down in cots, chairs and buggies. Children have their own rooms, their own screens and their own schedules. Independence is treated as something to train early. It isn’t your fault for following that. It’s what most of us have been handed. But a child’s biology still arrives expecting contact, and often what looks like neediness is simply that expectation not being met.

The Shift Map’s first shift for this signal is to let your child be near you while you get on with your life. Not to stop and give them your full attention every time, but to let them lean, sit close, come and go. Liedloff called it being in arms: the child carried along in the adult’s ordinary day. For an older child it might be the same room, the same task, the car journey. And when you do touch, to let it be easy and unhurried, not a hand that steers.

And if you’re reading this and thinking of the months your baby spent in a cot or a buggy, please don’t carry it as guilt. Almost none of us were shown another way, and many of us were told that holding a baby too much would spoil them. The need for nearness doesn’t close at six months, and the continuum never disappears. Nearness offered now, at whatever age your child is, still lands. It isn’t too late to be within reach.

So I wonder if it might be worth noticing, over the next few days, how much space there is between you and your child, and who keeps it. When they come close, does your body make room, or move away? And what might they be taking from it?

A moment to listen to yourself

How close am I?

This isn't a test and there are no right answers. It's a quiet moment to listen to yourself. Read each line and tick the box that feels most true for you at the moment. Try not to tick the one you think you should.

1. When my child leans on me or climbs close, I move or ask for space.

2. My touch is mostly for steering, hurrying or getting things done.

3. My child is in a different room from me for most of the evening.

4. My child clings or follows me about when I'm busy.

5. My body is tense when my child is close to me.

6. My child can be near me while I get on with what I'm doing.

7. My touch is easy and unhurried, when it's welcome.

8. I notice when my child comes close, and make room.
