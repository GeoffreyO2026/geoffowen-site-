Where Your Eyes Go

Where our eyes go, and what your child reads in them

“I’m listening”

Your child’s body registers where your eyes are. It always has.

Picture it. Your child comes to you with something that matters to them. You’re at the laptop, or the phone is in your hand, or you’re halfway through the washing up. You say “I’m listening” or “What?” and you carry on. And you probably are listening, in a way. But your eyes are elsewhere, your body is turned away and your attention is somewhere else entirely. And your child reads that far more clearly than they hear the words.

Where our eyes go is one of the nine presence signals I talk about in the Shift Map: nine presence signals, five innate expectations, four instinctive responses. It carries where your eyes and your attention actually are. When it’s off, a child tends to escalate: whining, interrupting, getting louder, until finally they are noticed. When it’s on, something quite simple happens. The child feels met, and they settle.

This piece looks at what the research says about this presence signal, and why it might be one of the easiest places to begin.

A note on language. When I say a child “reads”, “hears” or “expects” something, I don’t mean they are thinking it. A child isn’t weighing up who is in charge or whether you’re paying attention. These are biological processes, happening well below thought. I’ve put them into human words so we can talk about them.

What the eyes carry

In 1978 Edward Tronick and his colleagues ran one of the best known experiments in child development. A mother played face to face with her baby, smiling and responding. Then, on a signal, she kept looking at her baby but let her face go completely still and unresponsive. The babies tried everything to bring her back: smiling, reaching, calling out. When nothing worked, they became distressed, looked away and withdrew. At the time it was still widely assumed that babies didn’t really interact with their mothers in any meaningful way. Tronick showed they were doing it all along.

And it isn’t only babies. Tronick’s team later found that two and a half year olds showed the same pattern: more distress, looking away, trying to get closer to the mother and asking for her attention. The upset also carried over once the mother came back, with some children moving away from her. The researchers suggested the children were trying to make sense of something that simply didn’t fit what they expected from her.

In the first piece in this series we met the Cambridge study by Victoria Leong and Sam Wass. When an adult looked directly at a baby, the adult’s brain activity had a stronger influence on the baby’s brain, drawing the two into a shared state built for communication and learning. Eyes aren’t decoration in a relationship. They are one of the main ways two nervous systems find each other.

The phone in the room

The still face used to be something you set up in a laboratory. Now most of us carry one in our pocket.

In 2013 Jenny Radesky and her colleagues at Boston Medical Center quietly sat near families eating in fast food restaurants and wrote down what they saw. Of the 55 adults eating with young children, 40 used a phone or device during the meal, and almost a third used one throughout. The adults who were most absorbed tended to have more negative or less engaged interactions with the children, and sometimes responded harshly when a child tried to get their attention.

Researchers at Brigham Young University then turned the still face into a phone experiment. Instead of going blank, the parent looked down at a phone. The babies reacted in much the same way as in Tronick’s original study: more upset, fewer smiles, more self-soothing and more attempts to turn away. A 2025 study of mothers and young children found something similar, with repeated phone use affecting how well children managed their feelings and the quality of the time together.

Brandon McDaniel and Jenny Radesky gave all this a name: technoference, the everyday interruptions to time with your child caused by devices. In families with children of around three, they found that even small amounts were linked with more behaviour problems: frustration, hyperactivity, whining, sulking and tantrums. And they found it ran both ways. Parents who were stressed by a child’s difficult behaviour tended to retreat into their phones, and the retreat tended to make the behaviour worse. These are associations, and the studies can’t prove cause and effect. But the pattern is consistent, and most parents will recognise it.

And please hear this without blame. A phone can be a refuge for a tired parent, a moment of escape at the end of a long day. That makes complete sense. It’s simply worth knowing what it may cost.

Why your child gets louder

Every child comes into the world expecting immediate attention, not delayed. Not constant attention, and not being watched all the time. Simply that when they turn to you, you turn to them.

When that doesn’t happen, the biology does what it was built to do. It signals louder. Think of the shyest person you know, abandoned on a desert island, who suddenly sees a ship on the horizon. They’ll scream and wave and jump up and down, not because they’ve become a different person, but because everything in them is working to be seen. The whining, the interrupting, the “Mum, Mum, Mum, MUM” is often exactly that. It isn’t manipulation. It’s a flare going up.

And here’s the part that can feel unfair. Once the flare goes up, the behaviour tends to draw the very attention it was asking for, usually in the form of irritation. So the child learns that getting louder works, and the parent learns that their child is “attention seeking”. Both are caught on a roundabout neither chose.

The Shift Map points to a simpler way off. When your child comes to you, turn your eyes and your body towards them first, even briefly, before anything else. A few seconds of real attention, given early, often does what ten minutes of escalation never can.

Age and stage

Most of the research in this piece was carried out with babies and young children. The still face studies looked at babies in their first months and at two and a half year olds. The technoference research looked at children up to about five, with an average age of three, and the restaurant study at children under ten. There is much less research on older children and teenagers. But the principle, that a child reads where your eyes go before your words, doesn’t seem to switch off as they grow. These are illustrations, not scripts.

Age What tends to happen when the eyes are elsewhere What a settled presence might look like
Babies Bids for your face get bigger, then the baby turns away and withdraws. Your face turned towards theirs when they reach for you, even briefly.
2 to 5 Whining, tugging, “Mummy, Mummy, Mummy”, louder until noticed. Looking up from what you’re doing and turning towards them first.
6 to 10 Interrupting, showing off, or giving up and going quiet. A moment of real eye contact before you answer, then back to what you were doing.
11 and over Less asked for, more withdrawn, conversations kept short. Little research yet. Being visibly available, with the phone away, in the car or the kitchen.

What this means in your home

Most of us believe we are paying attention when we’re not. We’re listening with half an ear, eyes on the screen, mind on the next thing. And we tell ourselves it’s enough, because the words are going in. But what registers in your child isn’t whether the words went in. It’s whether you turned towards them.

This isn’t about putting your phone in a drawer forever, or giving your child your undivided attention every waking minute. Nobody can do that, and it isn’t what children need anyway. A child expects to be part of your life, watching you get on with things, not the constant centre of it. What they need is that when they reach for you, you’re actually there, even for a moment.

So I wonder if it might be worth noticing, over the next few days, what your eyes do when your child comes to you. Do they come up from the screen first, or do your words come first? Do you turn your body, or just your head? Not to judge yourself. Just to see. In my experience, this is often the quickest of all the presence signals to shift, and children tend to respond to it almost straight away.

If by any chance you’d like to explore this further, bring it to the monthly Q&A. I’d really like to hear what you notice.

A moment to listen to yourself

Where are my eyes?

This isn't a test and there are no right answers. It's a quiet moment to listen to yourself. Read each line and tick the box that feels most true for you at the moment. Try not to tick the one you think you should.

1. When my child talks to me, I answer before I look up.

2. My phone is in my hand or on the table during meals or play.

3. I say "I'm listening" while I carry on with what I'm doing.

4. My child has to ask several times, or get louder, before I respond.

5. I reach for my phone when my child's behaviour is getting to me.

6. When my child comes to me, I turn my eyes and body towards them first.

7. There are times each day when my phone is out of reach and out of sight.

8. I notice the moment my child wants to be seen, before they have to escalate.
