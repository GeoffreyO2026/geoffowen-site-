The Folly of “They’re Tired”

← All Insights

They’re tired. Two words, said in the supermarket, at the school gate, to a grandparent watching a meltdown unfold, and the moment they’re said, the room relaxes. Nothing more needs explaining. Everyone nods. I think this is one of the most convenient sentences in modern parenting, and I think convenience is exactly the problem with it.

Why This Excuse Is So Easy to Reach For

“They’re tired” closes a question before anyone has to actually answer it. It points at the clock, not the day, and it asks nothing further of anyone in the room. No parent has to consider what happened at nursery, what was rushed at breakfast, how many transitions were packed into the afternoon without a genuine pause between them. Tiredness, said out loud, ends the conversation, and I think that is precisely why it gets reached for so often. It is not wrong exactly. It is simply the smallest, easiest true thing available, offered instead of the larger, harder true thing underneath it.

What Tiredness Actually Is

A tired child is a real thing, and I am not arguing otherwise. But I think the word tiredness is very often a mask, sitting over something more specific.

A child arrives in the world already carrying a small set of expectations, wired in before birth, the same way lungs arrive already expecting air. Closeness. Prompt attention. A calm, confident adult clearly in charge. When enough of these go unmet across a day, a child’s body does not simply run out of battery on a fixed, predictable schedule. It starts coping, in whatever way is available to it, with something genuinely missing, and what we label tiredness is very often that coping response itself, not a cause in its own right.

Seen this way, a different question arises, not how do we get through bedtime faster, but what went missing today. And answering that question, once you know roughly where to look, tends to take considerably less effort, less time, and less patience than pushing a genuinely overwhelmed child through a bath and into bed while both of you run out of the little you have left.

Your child’s worst moment today was rarely about today at all. It was the point where an entire day, sometimes an entire week, finally became too much to keep holding.

A single word can end an inquiry just as effectively as a locked door.

The Employee Who Snapped on a Friday

Think of a colleague who finally loses their composure in a Friday afternoon meeting, sharp, uncharacteristically short with people, visibly worn down. It gets explained away easily. Long week. Everyone nods, sympathetic, and nobody looks any further. Almost nobody asks what actually happened across that week, the deadline moved without warning, the support that never arrived, the small disrespects that piled up quietly with nobody noticing any single one. Long week is true. It is also smoke, offered instead of the fire nobody wanted to go looking for.

A Different Lens

It’s six in the evening. Your child, who managed a full day of nursery, a rushed pickup, and no time to simply sit before dinner, refuses to get in the bath and starts screaming the moment you insist.

The thought that runs through most parents’ minds in that moment is immediate. They’re just tired, we need to push through bath time and get them down. What follows is a firmer voice, a faster pace, the very things least likely to help a nervous system that is already completely full.

Every child arrives already expecting certain things from the people around them, closeness, a calm and steady presence, enough space between one thing and the next to actually catch their breath. A day with too little of any of that does not simply produce tiredness. It produces a body that has been asked to keep coping, all day, with too little relief, until there is nothing left to cope with one more thing.

They weren’t refusing the bath. They were already completely full before you ever mentioned it, and what they needed was not a faster pace toward bedtime, but someone to finally slow down.

The Trap This Sets for Well Meaning Parents

“They’re tired” feels kind, even protective, offered instead of a harsher label like naughty or difficult. But I think the kindness is exactly what makes it so easy to stop there. It sounds like an explanation. It functions like a full stop, and a full stop is the last thing a genuinely overwhelmed child actually needs from the person who loves them most.

What Tends to Help Instead

I am not arguing that bedtime should move, or that tiredness is never real. I am arguing for one more question before the sentence gets said. Tired from what, specifically, today. Often there is a real, nameable answer, and once it is named, the evening that follows tends to look considerably different from simply pushing through to bedtime as fast as possible.

A Different Way of Reading Behaviour

“They’re tired” is one of the gentlest sounding excuses in modern parenting, and one of the most effective at stopping anyone from looking any further. Once you start asking what actually went missing before the coping began, I think it becomes very hard to reach for the phrase quite so easily again.
