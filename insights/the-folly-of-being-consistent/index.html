The Folly of Being Consistent

← All Insights

You have to be consistent, or they’ll learn to push the boundary. I think this is repeated so often in modern parenting that almost nobody stops to examine what consistency is actually being asked to mean, and I think, once examined, it turns out to be a considerably emptier instruction than it first appears.

A child can predict exactly what will happen and still not feel safe.

What Consistency Usually Means in Practice

In most parenting advice, consistency means applying the same consequence for the same behaviour every single time, regardless of context, regardless of what has been happening for that child that day, regardless of anything at all except the rule being followed exactly as written. I think this treats a child like a system to be calibrated, rather than a person whose behaviour is actually responding to something different each time it appears, even when it looks identical on the surface.

Why Rigid Consistency Isn’t the Same as Trust

A parent who enforces the same rule mechanically, no matter what, is being predictable. Predictability and trustworthiness are not the same thing, and I think this distinction matters enormously. A child can predict exactly what will happen and still not feel safe, because what actually builds safety is a parent who reads what is happening underneath the behaviour, not one who applies an identical response regardless of what generated it.

The Same Medicine for Every Patient

Think of a doctor who prescribes the identical medication to every patient, regardless of what is actually wrong with them, on the grounds that consistency is what makes a doctor trustworthy. Nobody would call that doctor reliable. Everybody would recognise, immediately, that real trust comes from an accurate diagnosis each time, not from an identical prescription applied without ever checking what is actually happening.

A parent applying the identical response to every instance of the same behaviour, regardless of what is actually driving it that day, is practising exactly this kind of medicine, consistent in form, and not actually reading the patient at all.

A Different Lens

Your child, who has never once refused to get in the car seat, suddenly fights it with everything they have, and the parenting book in your head says the rule is the rule, be consistent, don’t give in.

The thought that runs through most parents’ minds in that moment is immediate. If I make an exception now, they’ll learn resistance works, I need to hold the line exactly like always. What follows is the same firm response applied regardless of the fact that something about today is clearly different.

Every child arrives already expecting an adult who can actually read what is happening, not one running the identical script regardless of context. A sudden change in ordinary behaviour is very often a signal that something specific has shifted, and meeting it with mechanical sameness misses the signal entirely in the name of a principle that was never really about the child at all.

They weren’t testing whether the rule still applies. Something had genuinely changed for them today, and what they needed was to be read accurately, not processed through the same response as every day before it.

The Trap This Sets for Well Meaning Parents

Consistency feels like the responsible, disciplined choice, and I think that feeling is exactly the trap. It offers a parent the comfort of a fixed rule to lean on, rather than the unfamiliar task of actually reading each moment freshly. Reading a moment accurately is not extra labour. It can simply feel unfamiliar before it becomes instinctive, the way any new way of paying attention does at first. What actually costs more effort, over time, is holding a rigid rule in place against a child who senses, correctly, that it doesn’t fit what’s actually happening, and keeps testing it because of that mismatch. Rigid consistency can look like strength while quietly generating more resistance than it prevents.

What Tends to Help Instead

I am not arguing for randomness, or suggesting a child benefits from rules that shift unpredictably for no reason. I am arguing that what actually builds trust is not sameness of response, but sameness of underlying care, a parent who reads each situation honestly and responds to what is actually happening, even when that means the response looks different from yesterday’s.

A Different Way of Reading Behaviour

Being consistent, as usually taught, asks a parent to apply an identical response regardless of what is actually happening underneath a child’s behaviour. Once you start reading consistency as consistency of attention rather than consistency of consequence, I think it becomes very hard to reach for the mechanical version quite so easily again.
