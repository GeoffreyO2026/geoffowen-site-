Good and bad was never a scale. It was always a switch.

The Folly of Good Boy and Good Girl

← All Insights

If good girl is what she becomes the moment she shares nicely, what was she the rest of the day, when nobody was watching closely enough to say it?

That question rarely gets asked out loud, but it sits underneath the phrase every single time it is used. A child shares a toy. Good girl. Sits quietly through a meal. Good boy. Warm, well meant, entirely ordinary. And yet buried inside it is an assumption most parents have never had reason to examine, that goodness is not simply what a child already is, but something they arrive at, temporarily, through the right behaviour in the right moment.

What the Phrase Actually Implies

Say it often enough, attached to specific moments of compliance, and something quiet gets built alongside it. Not a description of character, but a kind of currency, handed out when a child’s behaviour suits the adults around them, and withheld, if only through silence, when it does not. Children are remarkably attentive to currency. They learn, quickly and accurately, what earns the label and what does not, and a great deal of what follows is shaped by that learning, not by any genuine internal sense of who they actually are.

It was never really about what was said, or left unsaid. It is a switch, not a scale. Acceptable or unacceptable. Welcome or rejected. Good or bad. On or off. There is no version of this where a child, or an adult for that matter, experiences themselves as sixty percent approved of. The nervous system does not deal in percentages. It reads a binary, and it reads it fast, long before conscious thought arrives to soften the verdict into something more nuanced.

This is why the pattern travels so completely into adulthood, into boardrooms, into one-to-ones, into every room where someone in charge decides who currently belongs and who currently does not. The switch was cached early, in childhood, long before anyone had the language to question whether the switch itself was ever a fair way to read a human being at all. And once cached, it runs quietly in the background for the rest of a life, at home, at work, in every room where someone senses, instantly, which side of the line they have just landed on, without a single word needing to confirm it.

A label handed out for compliance was never really describing character. It was teaching a child what they had to do to be welcome.

What Good Actually Means

I think it is worth asking, plainly, what good is even meant to describe in these moments, because I do not think anyone actually stops to answer it. Sharing a toy is not good in any absolute sense. It is one behaviour, in one moment, that happened to suit the adults nearby. A child who does not want to share is not bad. They are having an ordinary experience of not wanting to share, and I think calling the absence of that word a moral verdict is, frankly, an overreach most parents would reject if they saw it written down plainly like this.

For Your Own Good

There is a companion phrase doing related work from a different angle, and I think it deserves to be named just as directly. For your own good gets reached for, almost always sincerely, the moment an adult is about to do something to a child the child would not have chosen. And I think the phrase does something worth calling out honestly. It closes the question before it can be asked. Nobody has to define what good actually means, or whose version of good is being served, because the phrase has already declared the matter settled, on the adult’s terms, without the child ever being part of the conversation.

I do not think this is usually malicious. I think most parents reaching for it genuinely believe it. But sincerity does not change what the phrase actually does, which is end a discussion that a child, in most cases, was never invited into to begin with.

What This Actually Costs

A child raised on conditional goodness tends to carry something well past childhood, not I am good, full stop, but I am good when. When I comply. When I am convenient. When I do not make things harder for anyone. I think that when is one of the more quietly corrosive things a person can carry into adulthood, resurfacing as a difficulty simply resting in their own worth without constantly re-earning it through performance, usefulness, or being pleasant enough to justify taking up space at all.

What Tends to Help Instead

I am not arguing against encouragement, and I am not suggesting warmth be withheld from a child who has done something genuinely kind. What I am arguing for is specificity over verdict. Name what actually happened, that was kind, thank you for sharing that, rather than attaching the whole question of a child’s goodness to a single moment. And notice, honestly, how often for your own good gets used to end a conversation rather than have one, before assuming the matter was ever actually settled.

A Different Way of Reading Behaviour

Good boy and good girl are, in the end, small phrases carrying a much larger assumption, that a child’s worth is something earned through the right behaviour, rather than something already present, waiting to be trusted rather than proven. Once you start noticing how often that assumption sits quietly underneath everyday language, a lot of ordinary parenting moments start to look rather different.
