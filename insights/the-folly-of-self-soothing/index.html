The Folly of Self-Soothing

← All Insights

Put the baby down drowsy but awake. Leave the room. Let them find their own way to sleep. I think self-soothing is one of the most quietly misunderstood pieces of advice given to new parents, offered as a skill being built, when what is actually being taught is something considerably harder to reverse.

The crying stops not because the need has resolved, but because the infant has learned that crying produces no response.

What Self-Soothing Actually Asks of an Infant

A newborn, and for a long stretch after that, has no biological capacity to regulate their own nervous system. This is not a temporary inconvenience waiting to be trained out of them. It is where every human being genuinely starts, wired to co-regulate with a caregiver’s body before any capacity for internal calm exists on its own. Asking an infant to self-soothe is asking a system with no steering wheel yet installed to somehow find its own way home.

Why the Method Appears to Work

I want to be honest about why parents reach for this, because the method often does appear to succeed, and quickly. A baby left to cry, night after night, tends to stop crying sooner each time. Parents, exhausted and desperate for sleep, understandably read that as the skill being learned.

I think what is actually happening is considerably darker than a skill being acquired. The crying stops not because the need has resolved, but because the infant has learned, repeatedly, that crying produces no response. This is not calm. It is a very young nervous system giving up on the signal, because the signal has been shown, again and again, not to work.

The Stake Removed Before the Roots Exist

A young plant, staked to something sturdy while its own root system is still developing, is not being coddled. The stake is doing a job the plant genuinely cannot yet do for itself. Remove the stake too early, before real roots have had time to grow, and the plant does not become independent. It falls over, repeatedly, until something in it simply stops trying to stand upright at all.

An infant left to self-soothe is having the stake removed before any roots exist. The silence that follows is not the plant standing on its own. It is a very young system that has given up reaching for the support it still, genuinely, needs.

A Different Lens

It’s two in the morning, and your baby wakes crying for the third time. A book you trusted said to give it a few minutes before going in, to let them settle themselves.

The thought that runs through most parents’ minds in that moment is immediate. If I go in every time, I’ll create a habit I can’t break, I need to let them learn to do this on their own. What follows is a wait, watching the clock, listening to the crying continue, while every instinct in the room is pulling in the opposite direction.

Every infant arrives already expecting contact rather than separation, and prompt attention rather than delay, not as preferences but as genuine survival-level expectations wired in before birth. A baby crying in the night is not developing a bad habit. Their body is doing precisely what it was built to do, signalling that something expected is currently missing.

They weren’t learning to self-soothe. They were learning that the signal goes unanswered, and what they needed was not a few more minutes of waiting, but the same closeness their biology has been asking for since before they had a name for it.

The Trap This Sets for Well Meaning Parents

Self-soothing is presented as a gift, the early foundation of a child’s future independence and emotional regulation. I think this framing is precisely backwards. Genuine self-regulation later in life tends to grow out of having been reliably co-regulated early on, not out of having been left to manage overwhelming states entirely alone before the equipment to do so even existed.

What Tends to Help Instead

I am not arguing that a parent must respond to literally every sound instantly, or that a baby should never experience a brief pause before contact arrives. I am arguing against a method built specifically around extending that pause deliberately, night after night, in the belief that the resulting silence is progress. Responding promptly and consistently tends to produce, over time, exactly the security that self-soothing claims to be building, just by an entirely different, and considerably more honest, route.

A Different Way of Reading Behaviour

Self-soothing asks an infant to do something no infant is developmentally capable of doing, and calls the resulting silence success. Once you start reading a baby’s crying as a genuine signal rather than a habit to be broken, I think it becomes very difficult to leave a crying infant waiting quite so easily again.
